# Distinct ideas

Help the user discover an option they would have missed and decide whether it is worth trying. A familiar idea that solves the problem well is a useful benchmark. An unusual idea earns its place by changing an outcome the user cares about.

## Find what makes this brief different

Identify the person, the moment they get stuck, what they already do, and the observable improvement they want. Look for an awkward fact with consequences: a workaround somebody maintains, an idle asset, a rule that applies only sometimes, an exception that works surprisingly well. Separate observations in the brief from your assumptions. In a live workshop, invite people to jot down their own ideas privately before showing yours. When working unattended, use supplied notes or proceed with a stated assumption. Never invent interview findings.

Build a small comparison set from earlier conversation, the user's backlog, failed attempts, and alternatives the user supplied. Record the mechanism and why it was tried or rejected. If none is available, use likely conventional answers as a *predicted baseline* and say so. A request for “more” carries this set forward. With no access to past sessions, accept a pasted list or offer a short list the user can carry into the next session.

## Change how the outcome happens

Frame the problem at two different moments or from two participants' viewpoints. Generate candidates before ranking them. Select creative moves that suit this brief. For example:

- Find the point at which a bad outcome becomes inevitable. Move one decision to the last earlier moment when someone could still change it.
- Find a case where the constraint is already overcome. Identify the enabling condition and ask how someone else could acquire it.
- Borrow a working mechanism from a distant field. Name what is transferred, how it would operate here, and the condition under which the analogy breaks.
- Remove an assumed resource, reverse who initiates the action, or explore doing less. Check who would inherit any displaced work.

These are starting points. Vary the moves across rounds. Keep independent candidates long enough for a surprising one to compete on usefulness.

## Catch repeats before pitching

Describe each candidate in plain verbs: **who changes what, at which moment, through what mechanism, to produce which result?** Compare that description with the baseline and the other candidates. Merge ideas that would lead to essentially the same experiment. A new label, channel, or AI wrapper alone is weak evidence of a difference. A change of timing or actor counts when it changes the causal path. If a known idea becomes viable because an old constraint changed, say exactly what changed and present it as a revival.

Try removing the local fact on which a candidate relies. If its design and test would be identical for almost any user, refine it or keep it as the conventional comparison. Prefer a smaller honest shortlist to filling a quota with variants. When market originality matters, compare the strongest candidate to its closest documented alternative if sources are available. Describe the scope of that check. Unchecked originality stays unknown.

## Give the user a decision

Lead with the idea you would test first and the specific reason. For each finalist, make the local observation, mechanism, closest alternative, meaningful difference, and main tradeoff easy to find. Match the output length to the ask. Include the cheapest realistic experiment for the lead idea: who would try it, what they would compare it with, what to observe, and what result would make you drop it. Treat thresholds you propose as choices for the pilot. Include a harder, higher-upside direction when it offers a genuinely different bet.

In a group, give reviewers equal-length, unattributed idea cards before revealing which came from people or AI. Let the intended users judge usefulness. For a later round, retain a short record of the mechanisms already considered, the reason for each decision, and the evidence that could reopen it. Offer that record inline unless the user asks to save it.

Method inspiration: challenge framing, human-first brainstorming, divergent techniques, and blind selection from [Innovation Training's guide to AI ideation](https://www.innovationtraining.org/how-to-use-ai-to-generate-ideas/). The comparison set, mechanism check, and context-removal test address repeated suggestions across runs.
